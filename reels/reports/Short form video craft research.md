# Short-form video rules outrun their evidence

As far as these notes can show, a strong Reel, TikTok or Short in November 2026 opens on the result or the product inside 3 seconds, cuts out dead air and changes the picture every 2 to 5 seconds, carries word-timed captions of a few words in a heavy open-licence sans, is mastered near −14 LUFS with music well under the voice, runs 30 to 60 seconds, and closes on one specific call to action. Most of that is practitioner convention relayed by tool vendors, not measurement: no large-sample, frame-level study of top videos turned up, no platform publishes a pass mark, and five of the six note files rest on search summaries of pages nobody could open. The firm part is the platforms' own rules: keep Reels under 3 minutes (reach fell 49.3% above that across 2.7 million Reels), export with sound at 1080×1920, keep other apps' watermarks and text-covered frames out, and use only music a business is licensed for. The five reference Reels measured in this workspace sit inside the conventional band (3.6 to 4.9 cuts per 10 seconds, −14.2 to −14.8 LUFS, a comment-a-keyword ending on all four English ones), but they were hand-picked for style and show what those videos do, not what performs. For tools, the choice is an app stack at roughly US$40–65 a month for an editor plus licensed music, or a code pipeline (Whisper, FFmpeg, Remotion or HyperFrames, driven by a coding agent) with no software licence cost for a team of up to three. The parts for one system that serves long-form and short-form exist with documented hand-off points, yet nobody in the notes reports running one end to end. The notes are silent on sound effects, on how long the face can stay off screen, on animation timings for graphics, and on which hooks or calls to action produce bookings.

## What an editor can apply today, and how sure each rule is

This report synthesises six note files dated 24 November 2026. Five were compiled from web-search summaries: the researchers could open almost no source page and the shared search budget ran out before they finished, so every web figure below is as relayed by search and should be re-checked on the linked page before anyone publishes it or budgets on it. The exception is the tools research, which could open GitHub, npm and PyPI and read licence files, READMEs and release data directly. The sixth file is first-hand: five Instagram Reels the account owner chose as style references (two of the owner's own and three caption references from nrth.media), downloaded and measured in this workspace for cuts, speech, loudness, faces and text sizes ([reference measurements](../research_notes/Short%20form%20video%20craft%20research/reference_measurements.md)).

Each figure carries one of these labels, strongest first. **Measured here** means the five Reels, a tool run in this workspace or a file in this repository. **Official** means a platform's or licensor's own documentation or research, and **official via press** means a platform statement reported by trade press. **Study** means a dataset with a stated sample; a vendor study is one whose publisher sells a related tool. **Practitioner** means advice repeated across guides with no published data, **single claim** means one vendor, tool or creator, and **calculated** means the researchers' arithmetic on the above. **Unsourced** numbers are named only so they can be dropped. "Page unconfirmed" marks a figure the search summary quoted without saying which result page carried it. Pixel positions refer to a 1080×1920 frame with y measured from the top. ### No platform publishes a pass mark, so the yardstick is an account's own skip rate

"Best-performing" has an official meaning only in relative terms. Instagram's head, Adam Mosseri, named **watch time, likes and sends** as the top three ranking signals in January 2025, said likes weigh slightly more for followers and sends (shares by direct message) slightly more for non-followers, and told creators to watch the rates per reach, not the counts (official via press; [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-shares-algorithm-insights-2025/738034/)). Since about August 2025 Instagram also shows each Reel's **skip rate**, the share of views lost in the first 3 seconds ([Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-adds-retention-insights-reels/758464/)). YouTube shows "viewed vs swiped away" for Shorts and, since 31 March 2025, counts a public view whenever a Short starts or replays, keeping the older measure as "engaged views" ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/03/26/youtube-is-changing-how-youtube-shorts-views-are-counted)). TikTok lists its inputs without ranking them, so the popular claim that completion and rewatch come first rests on third-party guides ([Hootsuite](https://blog.hootsuite.com/tiktok-algorithm/)). No platform publishes weights or a hold percentage that unlocks distribution.

The few baselines with a stated sample are sobering. Business Reels lose **60–65% of initial views inside 3 seconds** (vendor study, about 140,000 Reels, January–June 2026; [Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/blog/instagram-reels-statistics/)), and brand Reels are watched for about four seconds on average (vendor study; [Dash Social](https://www.dashsocial.com/social-media-benchmarks/all-industries)). The median TikTok post gets **about 500 views** at every posting frequency (study, 11.4 million posts; [Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/how-often-should-you-post-on-tiktok/)), while another dataset's mean is about 32,900 (vendor study, 6.2 million videos; [Metricool](https://metricool.com/short-video-report/)): a few outliers carry the average. For Shorts, the only quantified link between an opening metric and results dates from April 2023: Shorts under 60% viewed rarely performed and the best sat at 70–90%, which the author called preliminary (study, 5,400 Shorts from 33 channels; [Paddy Galloway](https://threadreaderapp.com/thread/1646898356419981315.html)). The large studies even disagree on where a small account gets the most views: Metricool says TikTok, Socialinsider says Shorts ([Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/blog/tiktok-vs-reels-vs-shorts/)). The defensible benchmark for one account is therefore its own trailing median on each metric, with Instagram's trial reels, which show a video to non-followers first, as a built-in test audience (official; [Meta Newsroom](https://about.fb.com/news/2024/12/trial-reels-try-content-non-followers-first/)).

Four dated changes make older advice wrong. Shorts may run up to 3 minutes since 15 October 2024 ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/15424877?hl=en)). Reels may run up to 3 minutes since January 2025, with Instagram warning that longer ones "won't be recommended to new audiences"; its 2024 advice that Reels over 90 seconds hurt distribution is superseded (official via press; [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-officially-expands-reels-length-to-3-minutes/737766/), [Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/instagram-reels-length)). A Shorts view stopped meaning "someone watched" on 31 March 2025. And since 30 April 2026 Instagram removes accounts that mostly repost others' work from recommendations ([Instagram Creators](https://creators.instagram.com/blog/rewarding-original-creators-on-instagram)). TikTok's US feed has also run on a retrained model since January 2026, so earlier TikTok data may not transfer ([Forrester](https://www.forrester.com/blogs/tiktok-seals-the-deal-with-new-us-joint-venture)). One gap frames everything that follows: no large-sample study in the notes compares organic formats such as talking head, tutorial, story or faceless explainer, so any ranking of formats is opinion.

## Opening on the result is the only hook pattern with large-sample support

Platforms agree on the window, not on a target. TikTok's ad guidance says to state the proposition in the first 3 seconds and build the hook in the first 6 (official, advertising; [TikTok Business Help Center](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/creative-best-practices?lang=en)), and Instagram defines skip rate at 3 seconds. The references behave accordingly. The four English Reels finish their first sentence in **1.4 to 3.3 seconds**; the owner's tutorial (12,230 likes) says its title, "How to do a J-turn.", and cuts to the finished trick at 0.5 seconds, so the payoff is on screen inside the first second; and all three caption references open with "This is" plus the product. The Romanian venue Reel opens on a 7.5-second sentence. Five videos from three accounts show a habit, not an effect (measured here).

The one pattern every dataset repeats is to start with the thing itself. In a clipping vendor's correlational data from January–March 2026, clips that open on a product or outcome have the highest average views on TikTok (**6,037 per clip, about twice the lowest-ranked type**; 34,635 clips), expertise or credibility leads on YouTube (6,706; 8,254 clips), and a group combining direct address, intrigue and question leads on Instagram (952,227 clips) (vendor study; [OpusClip TikTok](https://www.opus.pro/research/best-video-hooks-tiktok), [YouTube](https://www.opus.pro/research/best-video-hooks-youtube), [Instagram](https://www.opus.pro/research/best-video-hooks-instagram)). Problem-first openings rank low on TikTok (2,034 average views), and so do shock or surprise openings (1,973), which conflicts with TikTok's own advice to build the hook on surprise ([OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/research/how-to-use-shock-surprise-hooks)). Two limits apply. The sample is clips cut by the vendor's own tool, averaging a few thousand views, so it describes ordinary accounts, not top creators; and nothing in it is causal. The other large study covers paid Meta ads only (578,750 creatives, September 2025–January 2026) and favours newness, sale, price and urgency openers during a season its publisher flags as promotion-heavy ([Motion](https://motionapp.com/library/research/creative-benchmarks-2026/top-hook-tactics)). Contrarian, list, warning and start-in-the-middle hooks rest on practitioner frameworks alone, and no word-for-word hook lines from named, measured videos were collected beyond the five references.

| Hook type | Evidence in the notes | Illustration (the researchers' wording, not from a measured video) |
|---|---|---|
| Result or proof first | Top type on TikTok and, as credibility, on YouTube (vendor study); a person plus the product in the first 6 seconds brought +5% 6-second views across 1,678 TikTok ads ([VidMob](https://www.vidmob.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/Vidmob-x-Tik-Tok-Hook-Analysis-.pdf)) | "This kitchen took four days and came in 1,200 under the quote. Here's how." |
| Question | Part of the top Instagram group, bundled with two other types (vendor study) | "Is your website quietly losing you bookings?" |
| Direct call-out of the audience | Same Instagram group; "label" and "if-then" call-outs in practitioner taxonomies | "Physios with a waiting list: this is for you." |
| Curiosity gap | "Intrigue" in the Instagram group; TikTok's ad guidance recommends suspense | "One line on your invoice decides whether you get paid on time." |
| Contrarian claim | Practitioner frameworks; the nearest measured category is "absurd statement" at 4,452 average views, platform unclear ([OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/research/repurpose-tiktok-for-youtube)) | "Posting every day is why your account isn't growing." |
| List promise | Nothing measured; repeated advice only | "Three things I check before I quote any roof repair." |
| Negative or warning | One anecdote; repeated advice only | "Stop sealing your deck until you've done this." |
| Story from the middle | Practitioner (foreshadow the payoff); thin support from paid ads | "So the client calls at 6 a.m. and says the whole floor is under water." |
| Shock or surprise | Near the bottom on TikTok in the vendor study, although TikTok's ad guidance recommends surprise | None offered |

Practitioner agreement is that the first frame, the spoken line and the on-screen headline must point at one subject and plant one question, and that the hook must work on mute (practitioner, from second-hand summaries of Kallaway and Jenny Hoyos; [Recapio](https://recapio.com/digest/how-to-create-a-killer-hook-impossible-to-skip-by-kallaway), [vidIQ interview summary](https://rosetta.to/u/vidiq/how-jenny-hoyos-actually-makes-viral-shorts)). They disagree on whether the text repeats the spoken line (Kallaway) or complements it ([Jupitrr](https://jupitrr.com/how-to/write-hooks-for-talking-head-videos)). TikTok's ad research supports getting text up early: videos with on-screen text in the first 7 seconds converted 43% better than videos that showed text later (official, ads, December 2021; [TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/blog/creative-that-drives-conversions)). Word counts for each layer are arithmetic, not findings: a line that must finish inside 3 seconds at 3–4 words per second is **7 to 12 words**, and a headline read in 1–2 seconds is about **3 to 7 words** (calculated). The references speak at 2.6 to 4.1 words per second (measured here), and a dated ad study found talk tracks at 4 or more words per second converting 19% better than 2–3 (vendor with platform, 2020; [TikTok for Business](https://www.tiktok.com/business/es/blog/vidmobs-data-driven-insights-to-supercharge-ad-creative-on-tiktok)).

After the hook, the advice is consensus without measurement: plant an open question and open a new one whenever one closes ([Adam Connell](https://adamconnell.me/improve-video-retention-social-media/), page unconfirmed), join beats with "but" and "so", write the hook and the last line first, and put the payoff in the final second (practitioner, second-hand; [Leaderonomics](https://www.leaderonomics.com/videos/box-of-chocolates/how-to-make-great-youtube-shorts-lessons-from-jenny-hoyos)). "Re-hook" has no agreed definition or interval in anything the researchers found, so any "every N seconds" rule is unsourced. So are "84% of final views are predicted by the third second" and the "65% three-second hold" target ([one carrier of both](https://dev.to/bulkdl/hook-engineering-the-science-of-the-first-3-seconds-in-short-form-video-2026-2jp9)), and "63% of the videos with the highest click-through rate state their message in the first 3 seconds", which blogs attribute to TikTok with no original document found ([Influencer Marketing Hub](https://influencermarketinghub.com/viral-tiktok-hooks-examples/), page unconfirmed).

### Calls to action: one specific ask, shown as text, judged on leads

The only hard call-to-action data is paid and dated. In TikTok's ad research a clearly stated text call to action converted **152% better** than videos that did not state the next step (official, ads, December 2021, validated globally except Brazil, Japan and North America; [TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/blog/creative-that-drives-conversions)), pairing it with a price lifted conversion 87% in Australian retail (official, 2023; [TikTok Works](https://www.tiktok.com/business/library/TikTok_Works_Performance_Creative_1_Pager.pdf)), and showing it in the opening frame beat showing it later by 44% (vendor with platform, 2020; [TikTok for Business](https://www.tiktok.com/business/es/blog/vidmobs-data-driven-insights-to-supercharge-ad-creative-on-tiktok)). The one organic study points the other way: across 540 Reels, those with a call to action performed worse overall, and only a prompt to comment was associated with higher interactions and reach (small vendor study, date unconfirmed; [Fanpage Karma](https://blog.fanpagekarma.com/?p=22627)). Both fit one rule: a single specific ask tied to the content and shown as text, not a list of generic asks bolted on.

Comment-a-keyword-for-a-message is the pattern the references use. All four English Reels end on it, for example "Comment the word FullSpec and I'll send you the link", and the three caption references pair the spoken line with a black pill near the top third of the frame that shows the keyword in capitals and then switches to a send icon and "In your DMs" (measured here). Instagram's position is two-sided. In June 2024 it said content that "explicitly asks for engagement through shares, comments, tags, or other actions" will not be recommended and gave "asking people to comment with a specific word, number, or emoji" as the example; days later it clarified that engaging in comments and messages "to connect personally, generate leads, or drive conversions is not considered to be engagement bait" (official via press; [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-says-using-certain-ctas-can-impact-post-reach/717732/), [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-clarifies-advice-single-word-ctas-longer-reels/718151/)). The same coverage noted that it is unclear how automated systems tell the two apart, and no on-record Mosseri statement about "comment X to get Y" posts was found. The mechanics are permitted under fixed limits: one private reply per comment, within 7 days, and nothing further until the person responds, which opens a 24-hour window (official; [Meta for Developers](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/business-messaging/instagram-messaging/features/private-replies)). The only conversion figures are a vendor's own (12–20%; [ManyChat](https://manychat.com/blog/conversion-rates-by-marketing-channel/)), and a write-up of another vendor's 57,052 delivery attempts concluded that no standard benchmark exists, because vendors define conversion differently ([Planoly](https://www.planoly.com/blog/do-automated-instagram-dms-work), page unconfirmed). Because comments are not among Instagram's three named signals, the keyword ask is a lead-capture device, not a reach device, and should be judged on leads. The safer wording says what the viewer will receive. The three caption references do ("...and I'll send you the link"); the owner's tutorial asks for the keyword without naming what comes back ("If you want to learn more stuff like this, comment MTB down below"), which sits closer to the example Instagram named.

For reach on Instagram the action that counts is the send, yet "shares" sit in Instagram's June 2024 list of explicit asks, and nothing measures whether asking for a send or a save produces one; saves are not among the three named signals. YouTube explicitly allows asking for likes, comments and subscriptions ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/3399767?hl=en)), but links in Shorts comments and descriptions are not clickable, so a pinned-comment link cannot work there ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13748639)). TikTok makes content ineligible for the For You feed if it tricks or manipulates people into engaging and says little about plain asks ([TikTok Community Guidelines](https://www.tiktok.com/community-guidelines/en/integrity-authenticity/)). The notes found no data on where a call to action performs best in an organic video, and none on which hooks or calls to action produce bookings or enquiries for a local or service business. The only support for such accounts is on the demand side, for example 65% of surveyed TikTok users saying they have discovered a local business, attraction or event there (platform-commissioned study, 2026; [eMarketer](https://www.emarketer.com/content/tiktok-drives-local-discovery-small-businesses-seek-new-customers)).

## Five measured references cut every 2.1 to 2.8 seconds; no study says that wins

No large-sample or frame-level measurement of shot length in top-performing talking-head shorts turned up. Practitioner rules of thumb cluster at one cut or picture change every **2 to 5 seconds**, with an aggressive camp at 1.5–3 seconds ([PodcastVideos](https://www.podcastvideos.com/17-smart-ways-to-edit-instagram-reels-for-better-viewer-retention.md), page unconfirmed), a relaxed camp at 3–6 seconds ([CutScore](https://cutscore.io/blog/how-long-should-a-shot-be), page unconfirmed), and dissenters who defend 8–10 second holds when something is developing in the shot ([Async](https://async.com/blog/how-to-edit-instagram-reels/)) or who say fast pacing "means removing wasted time, not changing visuals every two seconds" ([Jupitrr](https://jupitrr.com/how-to/edit-talking-head-videos)). The only platform data is TikTok's, for paid conversion ads: five or more distinct scenes brought a 171% conversion lift over fewer than five (official, ads; [TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/blog/creative-that-drives-conversions)). An academic preprint on about 10,000 brand TikToks found editing pace significantly associated with likes, comments, shares and saves, while prominent jump cuts showed weak or no association; the search summary did not show the direction of the pace effect ([arXiv 2606.16053](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.16053)). The five references sit at the brisk end of the practitioner band: **3.6 to 4.9 cuts per 10 seconds**, one every 2.1 to 2.8 seconds on average, with a median shot of 1.5 to 2.7 seconds and a longest shot of 4.6 to 12.7 seconds (measured here; the counts are a floor, because the detector can miss a jump cut inside one locked framing). The much-quoted turn away from hyper-fast "retention editing" is documented as creator statements about long-form YouTube, chiefly MrBeast in March 2024 ([Tubefilter](https://www.tubefilter.com/2024/03/04/mrbeast-editing-style-number-of-cuts-per-video/amp/)); no 2025–2026 measurement shows slower cutting winning or losing in shorts.

How the references cut matters as much as how often. The owner's J-turn tutorial alternates strictly: one numbered step said to camera, a cut to the action that shows exactly that step, then back to the speaker in a new position and framing, with every cut on a phrase boundary. Because no two speaker shots share a framing, it has no visible jump cuts. The venue Reel opens on action (the speaker chops wood while talking), then cuts a seated section every 1–1.5 seconds with small reframings and lays B-roll, meaning cutaway footage, as a horizontal band across the middle of the frame while the speaker stays visible above and below (read from frames at two per second; [reference measurements](../research_notes/Short%20form%20video%20craft%20research/reference_measurements.md)).

### Dead air, punch-ins and cutaways

Silence removal has a workable starting preset from the overlap of tool documentation and editor guides: treat audio below **−30 to −40 dB** as silence, cut gaps longer than **0.25–0.5 seconds**, and leave **0.05–0.25 seconds** of padding on each side of a cut ([auto-editor](https://github.com/WyattBlue/auto-editor), [FireCut](https://learn.firecut.ai/features/remove-silences), [Root Nation](https://root-nation.com/en/soft-en/lifehacks/en-silence-detection-settings-explained-volume-threshold-minimum-duration-and-buffer/), page unconfirmed). The guides split on padding, 0.05–0.1 seconds for the clipped social sound against 0.3–0.5 seconds for natural speech, and neither side cites retention data. Typical shrinkage is put at 15–25% of a talking-head take (single claim, page unconfirmed; [OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/best-ai-silence-removers)), with one worked example of 64 seconds cut to 48 ([Jupitrr](https://jupitrr.com/how-to/edit-talking-head-videos)). The three caption references are cut tighter than any of this: one or two pauses over 0.25 seconds per video and none longer than 0.38 seconds, where the owner's tutorial keeps a 2.88-second pause (measured here). One open-source editing skill adds rules worth copying: never cut inside a word, pad every cut edge by 30–200 ms to absorb timestamp drift, and put a 30 ms audio fade on every boundary (single claim, read in the repository; [video-use](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/browser-use/video-use/HEAD/SKILL.md)).

Editor guides hide jump cuts with an instant punch-in of about **8–15%** (scale 110–115%) on one side of the cut. Slower push-ins run from 100% to 115–125% over several seconds, and a quick move should land within about 12 frames, which is 0.4 seconds at 30 fps (practitioner; [FireCut](https://firecut.ai/blog/how-to-zoom-in-on-premiere-pro-a-step-by-step-guide-for-beginners/), [Pippit](https://www.pippit.ai/blog/capcut-alternative/capcut-zoom-in-effect)). Nothing found supports 130%. The venue Reel's median punch-in between neighbouring shots is ×1.10, inside that range (measured here).

For cutaways the best-documented figure is a single vendor test: B-roll covering roughly **35–50%** of a video lifted watch time by about 15–25% over talking-head-only versions, with diminishing returns beyond. The vendor sells an AI B-roll tool and showed no sample size or platform ([OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/does-b-roll-increase-watch-time)). Other guides say three to six cutaways of 2–4 seconds per minute, which is 10–40% coverage ([OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/ai-b-roll-generator)), or 30–60% ([Faceless](https://faceless.so/blog/retention-first-editing-boost-short-form-watch-time)), or 50–70% ([Fluxnote](https://fluxnote.io/guides/how-to-add-b-roll-to-talking-head-video)). The references go further than most of these: a face is on screen for only **20%, 24%, 42%, 51% and 64%** of the runtime (measured here), though three are sponsored product videos and one a sports tutorial, where the subject is the thing being shown. Practitioners also warn against a metronome, since a zoom at every exact 3 seconds stops registering ([CapCut](https://www.capcut.com/create/pattern-interrupt-technique-short-form-video), page unconfirmed). No evidence was found on how long the speaker's face can stay off screen before retention suffers.

Instagram sets the hard constraints on what sits in the frame. It says it aims to show fewer Reels that are "mostly covered by text", that "contain borders, logos, or watermarks", or that are "muted, blurry, or low resolution" (official; [Instagram Help Center](https://help.instagram.com/1525585517644948)). Mosseri has said the logo rule targets other apps' logos and "if it's your own logo, don't worry about it" (official via press, date not shown; [Social Media Today](https://www.socialmediatoday.com/news/instagram-clarifies-that-including-your-own-logo-on-a-reel-is-ok/730852/)). Branded intros appear in about 1% of 13.5 million analysed clips (vendor study; [OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/anatomy-of-a-viral-tiktok-2026)). On motion graphics the notes have almost nothing: no sourced entrance or exit durations, no easing rules, and nothing on panels, phone mockups or which looks practitioners now call dated. The caption references show one working answer: words arrive with a short blur that clears, blocks leave on a quick fade, and nothing bounces (measured here).

### Length: only the 3-minute ceiling on Reels is firm

The 2026 studies disagree on length because they measure different things. Buffer compared each account with its own baseline across about 2.7 million Reels (1 January–15 August 2026) and found reach **+7.4% at 90–179 seconds, +2.7% at 30–59 seconds and −49.3% at 180 seconds or more**, with differences under 90 seconds below three percent; engagement ran the other way and peaked above 3 minutes (study; [Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/instagram-reels-length)). Socialinsider pooled brand Reels and put the best views and engagement at 45–60 seconds (vendor study; the sample is given as 6 million Reels, though write-ups disagree on it; [Socialinsider](https://www.socialinsider.io/blog/instagram-reels-length/)). On TikTok, Buffer found videos over 60 seconds reaching 43.2% more people than 30–60 second ones and added that "video length is unlikely to be a make-or-break factor" (study, 1.1 million TikToks, March 2025; [Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/longer-tiktoks-get-more-views-data/)), a small scrape put the viral-tier median at 41 seconds (vendor, 1,449 videos; [OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/how-long-should-a-tiktok-be-2026)), and TikTok's ad guidance says 21–34 seconds ([TikTok Business Help Center](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/creative-best-practices), page unconfirmed). Shorts have the weakest evidence: a 2023 thread and a 2026 opinion survey of 507 creators ([Adobe Express](https://www.adobe.com/express/learn/blog/youtube-shorts-length-study)). The one large effect anywhere is the 3-minute cliff on Reels, which matches Instagram's stated rule; below it, length moves reach by single digits. For one edit published to all three, 30–60 seconds is the overlap (calculated), and four of the five references run 25 to 36 seconds (measured here). No 2025–2026 dataset reports completion or retention by length.

Endings rest on anecdote. A replay has counted as a public view on Shorts since 31 March 2025, but it does not add the "engaged views" that YouTube's monetisation uses, and vidIQ cautions that loops do not reliably multiply watch time ([vidIQ](https://vidiq.com/blog/post/double-youtube-watch-time-looping-shorts)). Sources disagree on whether Instagram counts replays as views ([Meta for Creators](https://creators.facebook.com/introducing-views-to-simplify-content)). Single creators report that cutting the tail helped: one second trimmed from the end raised a Short's retention from 83% to 88% ([Toptal](https://www.toptal.com/creator/post/youtube-shorts-length)). TikTok's own structure is hook, body, close, with the call to action inside the close ([TikTok Creative Codes](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-AU/creative-codes)). No data compares a spoken ending with a static end card.

## Captions run 48 to 85 px in open-licence heavy sans, and their lift is unproven

Word-timed captions in a heavy sans with a white fill and a dark edge are the 2026 baseline as tools and vendors describe it; in one vendor's 13.5 million clips, 80.2% carry captions and 78.6% animate them (vendor study; [OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/anatomy-of-a-viral-tiktok-2026)). What varies is the loud layer: all caps, a yellow or green highlight on the spoken word, emoji, bounce and sound effects, the package vendors call "Hormozi style" ([Kapwing](https://www.kapwing.com/explore/hormozi-style-captions-template)). Caption-tool vendors now call that package saturated and promote a quieter variant ([Blitzcut](https://blitzcutai.com/blog/tiktok-caption-trends-2026), [Joyspace](https://joyspace.ai/hormozi-editing-style-2026-analysis)). The shift is asserted, not measured: every source for it sells caption software, no statement from the named creators was found, and one vendor's own test (80 videos, no method published) found word-by-word colour emphasis beating plain auto-captions by 34% on watch time ([FluxNote](https://fluxnote.io/blog/ai-captions-that-improve-watch-time)). The supported reading is that word-level emphasis still helps and how loud it should be is unresolved. No count exists of what top accounts actually use.

The three caption references are a worked example of the quiet variant. They use two typefaces: a heavy grotesque with tight tracking for almost everything and a condensed italic serif for the words that carry the meaning. Each video has one accent colour, used only on the serif words (red #E64E4D in one, and in another a light blue that matches the sponsor's brand). Running captions show **1 to 4 words** in sentence case at about **48 px**, centred at about y=1174 (61% of the height), with an emphasised word dropping to its own line in the accent serif. The hook is a stacked, centred block of five lines in mid-frame (y 690–1227) at about 130 px, each word arriving as it is spoken; key lines are callouts of 76–82 px no wider than about 860 px; and the spoken call to action is set at about 104 px. Nothing has an outline or a box, and no text sits in the bottom quarter of the frame. These three Reels have 40 to 241 likes, so they were picked for how they look, not for reach (measured here; [reference measurements](../research_notes/Short%20form%20video%20craft%20research/reference_measurements.md)).

### Size, length and emphasis: vendor numbers against the measured references

Vendor numbers for a 1080×1920 frame converge loosely and conflict in places. On size, one seller gives **60–75 px** in bold or black weight and 70–85 px for karaoke styles ([Blitzcut](https://blitzcutai.com/blog/best-caption-size-tiktok-2026)); other guides give 48–70 and 60–80 px, and a vendor attributes a 48 px floor to Facebook ([OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/facebook-reels-caption-subtitle-best-practices)). The references' 48 px sits on that floor. On length, the vendor norm is **3–5 words** per caption and 2–3 for a faster feel ([OpenClip](https://openclip.app/guides/caption-styling-guide)), against another vendor's repeated 32–42 characters per line ([OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/youtube-shorts-caption-subtitle-best-practices)). The researchers measured the fonts bundled in this repository and found the two cannot both be followed: at 70–85 px a line 768–900 px wide holds only 14–26 characters, three to four words, and all caps costs 15–18% of that capacity (calculated; [caption notes](../research_notes/Short%20form%20video%20craft%20research/captions_fonts.md)). On the edge, one vendor specifies a 3–4 px black stroke with an optional soft shadow ([Blitzcut](https://blitzcutai.com/blog/how-to-make-captions-look-professional)), and vendors disagree on whether the "Hormozi" look itself uses a thick outline or a shadow with no stroke.

Two calculations explain the conventions. Highlight yellows from #F7C204 to #FDE047 differ from white neighbours by hue, not brightness (contrast 1.3–1.7:1), so a highlight that carries meaning needs a second cue such as weight or scale. Plain white text reaches 4.5:1 contrast only on backgrounds as dark as mid-grey #767676, which is why it needs a stroke, shade or box over skin, sky or walls (calculated; the 4.5:1 threshold is the researchers' recollection of the WCAG standard, not checked in the session). For the entrance, one community preset grows the word for 100–150 ms to 115–120% and shrinks it back ([kinetic captions skill](https://claudskills.com/skills/ffmpeg-kinetic-captions/SKILL.md)). At the references' pace of 2.6–4.1 words per second a word lasts about 250–380 ms, so an entrance longer than about 4 frames at 30 fps eats most of a one-word caption (calculated). No source gave a tolerance for caption sync against the audio, and none tested emoji, case, or stroke against box as performance variables.

### No short-form study shows captions lifting performance

No platform-published or peer-reviewed measurement of caption lift on Reels, TikTok or Shorts was found. "85% watch without sound" traces to a May 2016 trade report of a few publishers' Facebook feed analytics, an upper bound from the era of muted autoplay ([Digiday](https://digiday.com/media/silent-world-facebook-video/)), while TikTok's commissioned research says 88% of its users call sound essential (platform-commissioned study, 2021; [TikTok for Business](https://ads.tiktok.com/business/en-US/blog/kantar-report-how-brands-are-making-noise-and-driving-impact-with-sound-on-tiktok)). The best-documented numbers are old and not about short-form: Facebook's 2016 internal test, in which captioned video ads gained 12% view time ([3Play Media](https://www.3playmedia.com/blog/captions-increase-viewership-for-facebook-video-ads/)), and a 2019 survey of 5,616 US adults in which 69% said they watch muted in public and 80% said captions make them more likely to finish a video, which is stated intent, not behaviour ([Streaming Media](https://www.streamingmedia.com/Articles/News/Online-Video-News/80-of-Video-Caption-Users-Arent-Hearing-Impaired-Finds-Verizon-131860.aspx)). On a sound-on feed captions work as reinforcement and pacing more than as a substitute for audio, and nothing found supports a specific completion-rate promise. The formal accessibility standards for captions were not retrieved before the search budget ran out.

## All five references measure near −14 LUFS; bed level and sound effects stay unmeasured

Loudness has one documented reference and a convention built on it. YouTube has normalised playback to **−14 LUFS** since 2019 according to audio-engineering sources, not a YouTube page ([MeterPlugs](https://www.meterplugs.com/blog/2019/09/18/youtube-changes-loudness-reference-to-14-lufs.html)); LUFS is the loudness unit, and "integrated" means averaged over the whole clip. Meta confirms that Reels and Stories are loudness-managed at playback but publishes no number ([Engineering at Meta](https://engineering.fb.com/2023/04/11/video-engineering/high-quality-audio-xhe-aac-codec-meta/)), and TikTok publishes nothing. The common target of −14 LUFS integrated with a −1 dBTP true-peak ceiling is community consensus ([Mixing and Mastering AI](https://mixingandmastering.ai/blog/lufs-for-tiktok-and-reels)), with dissenters at −10 to −12 ([OpusClip](https://www.opus.pro/blog/best-loudness-normalizers)), near −16 ([Audio Gear Hub](https://audiogearhub.com/mix-audio-for-social-media/), page unconfirmed), and one unmeasured claim that TikTok does not normalise at all, in which case a −14 master would play quieter than louder neighbours ([Songbrain](https://www.songbrain.ai/guides/lufs-for-spotify-and-tiktok)). The first-hand evidence sides with the convention: all five reference files measure **−14.2 to −14.8 LUFS** integrated. Their true peaks are −0.3 to −0.8 dBFS, hotter than the −1 ceiling, and these are the files Instagram serves, not the creators' uploads (measured here). A mastering pass in this workspace took a clip from −16.9 to −13.9 LUFS with a −2.2 dBFS true peak, confirmed by a second measurement (measured here).

Music level under speech is advised anywhere from 6 to 30 dB below the voice, most often 15–24 dB: roughly **18–24 dB** quieter while someone speaks ([Overseer OS](https://www.overseeros.com/blog/background-music-volume-under-voiceover), page unconfirmed), about 15 dB as a first guess adjusted by ear ([Descript](https://www.descript.com/blog/article/how-to-set-the-perfect-audio-levels-for-video), page unconfirmed), and 20 dB in a W3C accessibility technique whose page the search returned without quoting ([W3C](https://www.w3.org/WAI/WCAG22/Techniques/general/G56.html)). The 6–12 dB "auto-duck" figure describes how far to lower music from its own level, not the final gap ([Mubert](https://mubert.com/tools/fuse/features/auto-duck), page unconfirmed). Both of the owner's Reels carry a clear music bed under the voice, but in the three caption references the speech is cut too tight for the bed to be measured, so the notes hold no measured bed level (measured here).

Licensing is where a business meets real constraints. TikTok says businesses cannot use its general music library commercially and directs them to its pre-cleared Commercial Music Library, whose tracks are cleared for TikTok only (official; [TikTok Ads Help](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article?aid=10005905)); music vendors add that this covers organic posts promoting a product, service or sponsor, including sponsored posts from personal accounts ([Soundstripe](https://www.soundstripe.com/blogs/why-can-i-only-use-commercial-sounds-on-tiktok)). Instagram says "certain business accounts and certain types of posts" lack the licensed music library and points to Meta's Sound Collection, licensed for Meta's products only (official; [Instagram Help Center](https://help.instagram.com/ipad-app/402084904469945)). YouTube's Shorts audio library avoids copyright claims, but the notes could not establish whether that covers brand or sponsored content ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/13486873?hl=en)). For one edit published to all three, only the voice track, original audio or independently licensed music is portable. A commercial plan from a stock-music library runs about US$17–30 a month in third-party price guides that conflict ([Activepieces](https://www.activepieces.com/blog/epidemic-sound-pricing-plans-and-costs-2026-guide.md)), and TikTok asks for a Music Usage Confirmation at upload for such music ([Epidemic Sound help](https://help.epidemicsound.com/hc/en-us/articles/26251264491794-How-to-Post-Commercial-Content-on-TikTok)).

Trending audio has no rigorous support. The best figure compares popular songs with less popular ones (17% more reach for songs already used in over 100,000 Reels, 2023; [Buffer](https://buffer.com/resources/trending-reels-on-instagram/), page unconfirmed), and nothing isolates a creator's own voice track, so for talking-head work the voice is the audio and a quiet licensed bed is optional. Instagram lists "muted" Reels among those it shows less, which makes a silent export a documented risk even for caption-led videos ([Instagram Help Center](https://help.instagram.com/1525585517644948)). Sound effects such as whooshes, pops and risers were never searched before the budget ran out, so the notes contain nothing on them.

### The code pipeline: transcript in, checked file out

The open-source projects converge on one architecture: a word-level transcript packed into compact text the model can read; an edit decision list; per-segment FFmpeg cuts with short fades; motion graphics rendered by Remotion or HyperFrames; captions applied last; loudness normalisation; a machine self-check on frames and meters; and a human approval point (the researchers' synthesis across repositories; [video-use](https://github.com/browser-use/video-use), [ghost-editor](https://github.com/kurbaitaev/ghost-editor), [cut-as-code](https://github.com/WhiteTowerAI/cut-as-code)). The video-use project states the central limitation outright: "The LLM never watches the video." Rendering frameworks are only the last third of that chain, so transcript quality drives edit quality. A catalogue lists 542 agent video repositories, graded only on their READMEs ([awesome-claude-video-skills](https://github.com/zhuyansen/awesome-claude-video-skills)), and since agent skills run commands on the machine, each deserves the review a new dependency would get.

The two render layers differ in licence and maturity. Remotion is source-available and free for individuals and for-profit organisations of up to 3 employees, counted as total company headcount including contractors on the project; above that it costs US$25 a month per person who makes videos, or US$0.01 per render with a US$100 monthly minimum (licence and FAQ read in the repository; [Remotion licence](https://github.com/remotion-dev/remotion/blob/main/LICENSE.md), [FAQ](https://github.com/remotion-dev/remotion/blob/main/packages/docs/docs/license/faq.mdx)). It ships official agent skills and a caption function that groups word timings into word-by-word pages ([Remotion skills](https://github.com/remotion-dev/skills)). HyperFrames, from HeyGen, is Apache 2.0 with no fees or thresholds and renders HTML with GSAP animation, but it is pre-1.0, with 672 published versions since March 2026 and 725 issues that include render hangs, so a production setup should pin a version ([HyperFrames](https://github.com/heygen-com/hyperframes)). Its media skill routes to HeyGen's signed-in services unless local tools are requested ([HyperFrames media skill](https://github.com/heygen-com/hyperframes/blob/main/skills/media-use/SKILL.md)). Both ran in this workspace: Remotion 4.0.526 rendered an 11-second 1080×1920 video in 38 seconds and HyperFrames 0.9.88 a 3-second test in 8 seconds, each after two fixes for a cloud container (fonts and scripts stored locally instead of fetched from a CDN, and the pre-installed headless Chrome). In the same run faster-whisper medium transcribed 17 seconds of speech in 13.6 seconds on four CPU cores, and an audio-based cut took a 17.2-second clip to 11.0 seconds with every cut edge passing its check (measured here).

Practitioner reports agree on where this pays. It suits repeatable, caption-heavy formats with human approval; one tutorial author puts break-even at about ten videos of the same structure and calls a conventional editor faster for a single video (single claim, page unconfirmed; [Justin McKelvey](https://justinmckelvey.com/blog/how-to-edit-video-with-claude)). In one like-for-like test of 16 skills on a 15-second promo, a no-skill baseline took 4.4 minutes, Remotion's skills 4.5 and HyperFrames 23.5, and the tester concluded that a skill "earns its place with a look, a workflow, or footage to edit" (single tester, no footage involved; [awesome-claude-video-skills](https://github.com/zhuyansen/awesome-claude-video-skills)). Reported failures are taste and time: graphics covering the speaker's face, captions drifting or breaking mid-phrase, a full re-render for one caption typo, and 15–25 minutes to render a 60-second reel on a laptop (single claims; [ChatCut](https://chatcut.io/blog/can-claude-edit-videos), page unconfirmed; [ghost-editor](https://github.com/kurbaitaev/ghost-editor)). No independent production report with volumes, hours and defect rates was found. The first reference the owner supplied, a YouTube Short by David Roberts titled "How Claude edits all my Reels: the full system", describes exactly this kind of pipeline: Claude Code with Whisper, FFmpeg, HyperFrames and yt-dlp, a style taught from 3–5 measured reference Reels, and a numbered edit plan approved before rendering. Its author co-founded the paid idea-finding product the system recommends, so the free system is also marketing, and the researchers could not confirm what that product costs (read first-hand from the Short's public description and the creator's linked document; the video itself could not be downloaded).

Word timing is the foundation, and it is weaker outside English. The Whisper variants share weights but derive word times differently, and OpenAI labels its own word timestamps "experimental" ([Whisper](https://github.com/openai/whisper/blob/main/whisper/transcribe.py)). The only comparative figure, a vendor's benchmark on read English, puts WhisperX at about 65 ms mean word-boundary error ([CrisperWhisper](https://github.com/nyrahealth/CrisperWhisper)), which is two frames at 30 fps: tolerable for word-by-word captions and risky for cuts on a word boundary, hence the padding rule above. OpenAI's own chart prints word error rates of about **5–6% for German and 8–11% for Romanian** (the vendor's evaluation, read off the figure by the researcher; [Whisper language breakdown](https://github.com/openai/whisper/blob/main/language-breakdown.svg)), so Romanian captions need a human proofreading pass every time. Tool defaults assume English (`medium.en`, `small.en`, `whisper-tiny`), so a Romanian or German pipeline must set a multilingual model and the language explicitly, and WhisperX aligns Romanian with a community model of unknown quality ([WhisperX alignment code](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/m-bain/whisperX/HEAD/whisperx/alignment.py)). No benchmark measures word timing in either language.

### Long-form and short-form in one system: the join points exist, the whole is untested

The notes were researched for short-form vertical video. They do not evaluate any long-form editing workflow, and no note describes a single system that does both. What they document is the set of AI tools by job, in the table above, and three points where those tools connect. The first is long-to-short clipping: OpusClip sells it as a service, and the open-source claude-shorts takes a long video, gets word timestamps from faster-whisper, has the model score 8–12 candidate segments on five weighted criteria (hook strength 0.30, standalone coherence 0.25, emotional intensity 0.20, value density 0.15, payoff 0.10), reframes to 1080×1920 with face tracking and renders captions in Remotion ([claude-shorts](https://github.com/AgriciDaniel/claude-shorts)). The second is hand-off to a conventional editor for the long cut: auto-editor exports its silence-removed timeline for Premiere Pro, DaVinci Resolve and Final Cut Pro ([auto-editor](https://github.com/WyattBlue/auto-editor)), Remotion's render skill exports OpenTimelineIO timelines ([Remotion skills documentation](https://github.com/remotion-dev/remotion/blob/main/packages/docs/docs/ai/skills.mdx)), and AutoCut runs as a plugin inside Premiere and Resolve. The third is the shared transcript: every pipeline starts from one word-timed transcript, which can serve both edits.

Put together, the system those parts imply runs as follows: transcribe the long recording once; remove silences and repeated takes from the transcript; send that timeline to a desktop editor, where a person finishes the long version; let the agent pick short candidates from the same transcript, then reframe, caption and master them in the code pipeline; and gate both outputs on the same checks and a human approval. This is a design inference from the tools' documented inputs and outputs, not something any source reports running. The evidence against doing the long version entirely in code is thin but points one way. HyperFrames' maintainer says it "isn't trying to replace a pro NLE like Premiere or Resolve" ([HyperFrames issue 318](https://github.com/heygen-com/hyperframes/issues/318)); one write-up reports local renders of 30–60 minutes for a 10-minute captioned video (single claim, page unconfirmed; [Shweta Creates](https://shwetacreates.substack.com/p/how-to-edit-videos-with-claude-code)); and cut-as-code handles only chronological clips from one source ([cut-as-code](https://github.com/WhiteTowerAI/cut-as-code)).

### Checks, export and reference videos

Before delivery, the checklists and open-source skills converge on a gate that a machine can mostly run: loudness measured on the final file against −14 LUFS and −1 dBTP; no internal silence, black or frozen frames beyond set thresholds (one production tool fails a file on a silence gap over 2.5 seconds, black over 1.5 seconds or a freeze over 3.0 seconds; [shorts-qc](https://github.com/DangTruong96/shorts-qc)); a frame inspected at every cut for flashes, jumps and hidden captions ([video-use](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/browser-use/video-use/HEAD/SKILL.md)); caption text proofread by a person; a re-transcription of the final audio to catch sync errors ([Turntake](https://turntake.app/learn/video-ad-qa-checklist), page unconfirmed); and one human watch-through with sound on a phone. Four independent projects build in a human approval step before the final render. FFmpeg ships the detectors, but its defaults (2 seconds for silence, black and freeze, with silence set at −60 dB) are looser than those production thresholds and its loudness filter defaults to −24 LUFS, so each target has to be set ([FFmpeg](https://github.com/FFmpeg/FFmpeg/blob/master/doc/filters.texi)).

One export preset satisfies every published requirement: 1080×1920, H.264 High Profile in MP4, 10–20 Mbps variable bitrate, AAC at 48 kHz, under 300 MB (calculated from [YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/1722171?hl=en), [Meta developer documentation](https://developers.facebook.com/documentation/instagram-platform/instagram-graph-api/reference/ig-user/media) and [TikTok developer documentation](https://developers.tiktok.com/docs/en/content-posting-api-media-transfer-guide?enter_method=left_navigation)). Frame rate is the one conflict: Instagram's Help Center says 30 fps minimum while its API accepts 23–60 fps, and three of the five references are served at 24 or 23.976 fps (measured here). TikTok publishes no bitrate recommendation for organic uploads, no source found a benefit from very high upload bitrates, and Mosseri has said Instagram serves lower-quality encodes to videos that stop getting views ([TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2024/10/27/instagram-is-lowering-video-quality-for-unpopular-videos)). On the rules as found, a filmed talking head with captions and motion graphics needs no AI label on any of the three platforms; a label becomes necessary when a realistic synthetic element is added, such as a cloned voice or generated footage that could pass as real ([YouTube Help](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/14328491), [TikTok Newsroom](https://newsroom.tiktok.com/more-ways-to-spot-shape-and-understand-ai-content?lang=en), [Instagram Help](https://help.instagram.com/761121959519495)).

Studying reference videos, the step every agent workflow starts with, is fragile. From this cloud workspace YouTube refused every download request and Instagram allowed five of six anonymous downloads after spaced retries (measured here), and yt-dlp's maintainers warn that using account cookies risks a ban ([yt-dlp wiki](https://github.com/yt-dlp/yt-dlp/wiki/Extractors)). Reference clips are best supplied by a person from a home connection; what the platforms' terms say about downloading was not verified. Official routes return metadata, not files: TikTok's Creative Center is free and public ([TikTok](https://ads.tiktok.com/help/article/creative-center)), while Meta's Content Library is open to vetted researchers only. Three costs are missing from every total: the coding-agent subscription a code pipeline needs, which was not priced in the session; stock footage and templates; and labour hours per finished short, which no source measured.

## Conclusion

The useful dividing line is not app against code or loud captions against quiet ones. It is constraint against hypothesis. Wherever a platform states a rule (the 3-minute ceiling, originality, watermarks, music licences, AI labels), the large datasets agree with it. Wherever the platform is silent (cut rate, caption style, length below the ceiling, bed level, where the call to action goes), the evidence is weak or conflicting. An editor can lock the first group into a checklist and treat everything else as a default to be tested against the account's own skip rate, sends per reach and leads, with trial reels as the control audience.

The cheapest evidence in the notes was also the most informative. Five measured videos confirmed one convention, the −14 LUFS master, and departed from several vendor norms an editor would otherwise apply on trust: captions at 48 px instead of 60–75, a face on screen for as little as a fifth of the runtime, and delivery at 24 fps. The same measurement run on videos chosen for results instead of looks, an account's best and worst by skip rate or sends per reach, would produce the dataset the public sources lack. Two decisions will shape the rules more than any further reading: whether videos may ever run as ads, which moves the caption line from about y=1600 up to y=1248, and whether a human proofreading pass for Romanian captions is built into the workflow.
