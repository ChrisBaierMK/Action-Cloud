## 1. What do top creators and professional short-form editors use in 2025–2026 for each step (editing, captions, clipping, silence removal, B-roll, music, templates), at what price, and with what availability or licensing problems?

### Takeaway
The usable evidence says CapCut leads among creators making vertical video on phones while Premiere stays the leading choice among agency editors, surrounded by single-purpose AI tools that each cost about US$10–40 a month (Submagic or Captions for captions, OpusClip for clipping, AutoCut/FireCut/Gling/TimeBolt for silence removal). The usage-share numbers contradict each other (CapCut "54%" versus "4%"), I found no interview in which a named large creator or editor states their tools, and CapCut carries the largest non-price risks: a broad content licence in its terms and a history of US availability disruption.

### Inferences
- The "54%" and "4%" CapCut figures probably measure different populations (primary editor for vertical video among creators versus share of paid desktop licences), so neither should be quoted as a market share without its denominator.
- Three app-based stack shapes recur in the pricing material: CapCut alone; a desktop editor (Premiere, Resolve or Final Cut) plus a silence-removal plugin plus a caption tool; and Descript as a text-based all-in-one. I did not find evidence on which shape top performers favour.
- Quota-priced tools translate into a cost per video: Submagic is about US$1.27 per video on Starter (19 ÷ 15), US$0.98 on Pro and US$0.69 on Business; OpusClip is about US$0.10 per minute of source video (15 ÷ 150).
- For a business that films its own staff, the CapCut licence question is about face and voice footage, not just music. A desktop editor or a local code pipeline avoids uploading raw footage at all.
- Adobe's 57% figure (AI output needs moderate or extensive editing) fits the practitioner view in question 2 that AI tools do the first pass, not the finish.

## 2. AI-agent and code-based pipelines as of November 2026: which workflows, agent skills and repositories exist, what do they do well and badly, what do practitioners report, and what are the licence terms?

### Takeaway
A large open ecosystem now exists: agents target Remotion (source-available, free up to 3 people, official Agent Skills) or HeyGen's HyperFrames (Apache-2.0, HTML plus GSAP, first published March 2026, 88.8k GitHub stars) as the render layer, use FFmpeg for cutting and encoding, and take word timings from Whisper-family or hosted speech-to-text; one catalogue lists 542 agent video repositories. Practitioner evidence agrees that this works for repeatable, caption-heavy formats with human approval points, and that the weak spots are taste, the agent's inability to watch or listen, long render times and unstable tooling (HyperFrames is pre-1.0 with 672 releases in eight months).

### Cited Findings

#### Remotion: licence and official agent support
- Remotion's licence makes it free for an individual, a for-profit organisation with up to 3 employees, a non-profit, or anyone "evaluating whether Remotion is a good fit" and not yet using it commercially; everyone else needs a Company License. Copying or modifying Remotion code to sell, rent, license, relicense or sublicense a derivative is not allowed. **[E1]** — [Remotion LICENSE.md](https://github.com/remotion-dev/remotion/blob/main/LICENSE.md)
- Eligibility "depends on your company's total headcount", not the number of people using Remotion; contractors who help operate the software on the same project count toward the 4-person threshold. **[E1]** — [Remotion licence FAQ](https://github.com/remotion-dev/remotion/blob/main/packages/docs/docs/license/faq.mdx)
- An agency that hands the client only the finished file counts only its own headcount; if the client owns or operates the project, both headcounts are added. **[E1]** — [Remotion licence FAQ](https://github.com/remotion-dev/remotion/blob/main/packages/docs/docs/license/faq.mdx)
- Company pricing: "Remotion for Creators" costs "$25/month per person who makes videos with Remotion"; "Remotion for Automators" costs "$0.01 per render" with "a minimum spend of $100/month"; Enterprise has a minimum spend of US$500. A render is "the successful generation of a video, audio, GIF, still image or image sequence", and Studio or Player previews do not count. **[E1]** — [Remotion licence FAQ](https://github.com/remotion-dev/remotion/blob/main/packages/docs/docs/license/faq.mdx)
- "No Seat is needed for: AI agents. Only the people using them need a Seat"; a Creators seat covers people "whether they write code, prompt an AI agent or edit in the Remotion Studio". Videos already produced stay usable after cancelling. **[E1]** — [Remotion licence FAQ](https://github.com/remotion-dev/remotion/blob/main/packages/docs/docs/license/faq.mdx)
- Remotion 5.0 will make telemetry mandatory for Automators customers; it stays voluntary for free and Creators users. **[E1]** — [Remotion licence FAQ](https://github.com/remotion-dev/remotion/blob/main/packages/docs/docs/license/faq.mdx)
- Current release is 4.0.546 (23 November 2026) with about 4.5 million npm downloads in the week of 16–22 November; the npm licence field reads "SEE LICENSE IN LICENSE.md". **[E1]** — [npm registry: remotion](https://registry.npmjs.org/remotion/latest), [npm downloads API](https://api.npmjs.org/downloads/point/last-week/remotion)
- Remotion maintains official Agent Skills for Claude Code, Codex, Cursor, Kimi Code and OpenCode, installed with `npx remotion skills add`; the set includes `/remotion-best-practices`, `/remotion-captions`, `/remotion-render` (which also exports OpenTimelineIO timelines) and `/remotion-license`. **[E1]** — [Remotion skills docs](https://github.com/remotion-dev/remotion/blob/main/packages/docs/docs/ai/skills.mdx), [remotion-dev/skills](https://github.com/remotion-dev/skills)
- "The hosted Remotion MCP server was shut down on October 26, 2026 and is no longer available"; the docs point to the `/remotion-docs` skill instead. **[E1]** — [Remotion MCP docs](https://github.com/remotion-dev/remotion/blob/main/packages/docs/docs/ai/mcp.mdx)
- Remotion's coding-agent guide notes that "Most coding agents require a paid subscription". **[E1]** — [Remotion coding-agents docs](https://github.com/remotion-dev/remotion/blob/main/packages/docs/docs/ai/coding-agents.mdx)

### Inferences
- The projects converge on one architecture: word-level transcript → compact text the model can read → edit decision list → per-segment FFmpeg cuts with short fades → motion graphics rendered by Remotion or HyperFrames → captions last → loudness normalisation → machine self-check on frames and meters → human approval. Rendering frameworks are only the last third of that chain, so transcript quality drives edit quality.
- For a team of three or fewer, Remotion costs nothing; the licence only becomes a decision at a total company headcount of four, where it is US$25 per month per person who makes videos.
- HyperFrames' 672 releases in roughly eight months (about 2.7 per day) and its regression issues suggest pinning a version for production and re-testing before upgrading.

- Skill catalogues grade READMEs, not code. Agent skills run commands on the machine, so each one deserves the review a new dependency would get.
- The media skill's default toward HeyGen's signed-in services means "free and local" has to be requested explicitly when using HyperFrames.

## 3. Speech-to-text for word-level timing: Whisper variants, their word-timestamp precision and failure modes, and accuracy in languages other than English (Romanian, German)

### Takeaway
The variants share the same Whisper weights but derive word times differently: openai-whisper, faster-whisper and mlx-whisper read them from cross-attention (OpenAI labels this "experimental"), whisper.cpp offers experimental token timestamps or an optional DTW mode, and WhisperX re-aligns with a separate wav2vec2 model per language. The only comparative figure found puts WhisperX at about 65 ms mean word-boundary error on read English speech (a vendor's benchmark), and one production skill pads every cut by 30–200 ms to absorb 50–100 ms of drift. German is a top-tier Whisper language (about 5–6% word error rate); Romanian is mid-tier (about 8–11%) and depends on a community alignment model in WhisperX.

### Cited Findings

#### The variants
- openai-whisper (MIT, release 20250625): `--word_timestamps` is described as "(experimental) extract word-level timestamps and refine the results based on them". The code runs dynamic time warping over median-filtered cross-attention weights and contains explicit "hack" steps that truncate over-long words at sentence and segment boundaries. **[E1]** — [whisper transcribe.py](https://github.com/openai/whisper/blob/main/whisper/transcribe.py), [whisper timing.py](https://github.com/openai/whisper/blob/main/whisper/timing.py), [PyPI: openai-whisper](https://pypi.org/project/openai-whisper/)
- The `turbo` model is "an optimized version of `large-v3`" (809 M parameters, about 6 GB VRAM, about 8× the speed of `large`) and is not trained for translation. **[E1]** — [Whisper README](https://github.com/openai/whisper)
- faster-whisper (MIT, 1.2.1, October 2025) reimplements Whisper on CTranslate2 and claims to be "up to 4 times faster than openai/whisper for the same accuracy while using less memory" (13 minutes of audio: 2m23s versus 1m03s, or 17 s batched, on an RTX 3070 Ti). It exposes `word_timestamps=True` and a Silero voice-activity filter that by default removes only silences longer than 2 seconds. **[E1]** — [faster-whisper README](https://github.com/SYSTRAN/faster-whisper), [PyPI: faster-whisper](https://pypi.org/project/faster-whisper/)
- whisper.cpp (MIT) runs on Apple Silicon (Metal, Core ML), CUDA and Vulkan; its README labels `-ml 1` output "Word-level timestamp (experimental)", and the CLI adds `-sow` (split on word), `-dtw MODEL` ("compute token-level timestamps") and Silero VAD flags. The large model needs about 3.9 GB of memory. **[E1]** — [whisper.cpp README](https://github.com/ggml-org/whisper.cpp), [whisper.cpp CLI options](https://github.com/ggml-org/whisper.cpp/blob/master/examples/cli/README.md)
- WhisperX (BSD-2-Clause, 3.8.6, May 2026) adds VAD pre-segmentation, batched inference (about 70× real time with large-v2), wav2vec2 forced alignment for word times and optional pyannote diarisation; it needs under 8 GB of GPU memory for large-v2 and can run on CPU with `--compute_type int8`. **[E1]** — [WhisperX README](https://github.com/m-bain/whisperX), [PyPI: whisperx](https://pypi.org/project/whisperx/)
- mlx-whisper (MIT, 0.4.3, August 2025) runs Whisper on Apple silicon with `word_timestamps=True`; its default model is `whisper-tiny`, so a larger model has to be chosen explicitly. **[E1]** — [mlx-whisper README](https://github.com/ml-explore/mlx-examples/tree/main/whisper), [PyPI: mlx-whisper](https://pypi.org/project/mlx-whisper/)
- stable-ts (MIT, 2.19.1) post-processes Whisper timestamps with silence suppression, regrouping and a `refine()` step, wraps faster-whisper and MLX backends, and can align a known transcript to audio with `align()`; its README states "Development on this repository is indefinitely paused." **[E1]** — [stable-ts README](https://github.com/jianfch/stable-ts), [PyPI: stable-ts](https://pypi.org/project/stable-ts/)
- whisper-timestamped computes word times by DTW on cross-attention, adds per-word confidence and a disfluency detector, and is copyleft (PyPI lists GPLv3). **[E1]** — [whisper-timestamped README](https://github.com/linto-ai/whisper-timestamped), [PyPI: whisper-timestamped](https://pypi.org/project/whisper-timestamped/)

#### Precision figures
- Mean word-boundary error on TIMIT (read English), as published by the CrisperWhisper authors: CrisperWhisper 2.0 29.6 ms, ElevenLabs Scribe v2 51.3 ms, Deepgram Nova-3 63.3 ms, WhisperX 64.8 ms, NVIDIA Canary 85.5 ms. Not independently verified; CrisperWhisper's weights are non-commercial. **[E4, vendor benchmark, opened]** — [CrisperWhisper README](https://github.com/nyrahealth/CrisperWhisper)
- video-use instructs the agent to pad every cut edge by 30–200 ms "to absorb 50–100ms of timestamp drift", working from ElevenLabs Scribe word times. **[E4, practitioner rule, opened]** — [video-use SKILL.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/browser-use/video-use/HEAD/SKILL.md)
- HyperFrames' docs report 6.05% word error rate for Parakeet against 7.44% for whisper.cpp, and 5–10× the speed, without naming the test set. **[E4, vendor docs, opened]** — [HyperFrames audio reference](https://github.com/heygen-com/hyperframes/blob/main/skills/media-use/references/audio.md)
- The WhisperX paper argues Whisper's own timestamps are "too unreliable" and that buffered long-form decoding is "prone to severe drifting"; the README says native timestamps are utterance-level and can be off by several seconds. **[E1 for the README; paper seen as snippet]** — [WhisperX paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.00747), [WhisperX README](https://github.com/m-bain/whisperX)

#### Known failure modes
- Hallucination and repetition: OpenAI's model card says predictions "may include texts that are not actually spoken in the audio input" and that the architecture is "prone to generating repetitive texts", likely worse in lower-resource languages. The CLI offers `--hallucination_silence_threshold` and notes that disabling `--condition_on_previous_text` makes the model "less prone to getting stuck in a failure loop". **[E1]** — [Whisper model card](https://github.com/openai/whisper/blob/main/model-card.md), [whisper transcribe.py](https://github.com/openai/whisper/blob/main/whisper/transcribe.py)
- Early starts and late ends around pauses: stable-ts exists because word starts can be predicted well before the word is spoken and ends can run past the speech. **[E1]** — [stable-ts README](https://github.com/jianfch/stable-ts)
- Dropped fillers shift timing: Whisper often omits disfluencies, which can move the next word's timestamp; a transcript-driven editor therefore cannot find "um" to cut it. **[E1]** — [whisper-timestamped README](https://github.com/linto-ai/whisper-timestamped), [MakeMyClip editor README](https://github.com/MakeMyClip/editor)
- Timestamp-token methods can drift, especially over music, and Whisper's segment timestamps are coarse. **[E4, a competing project's claim]** — [whisper-timestamped README](https://github.com/linto-ai/whisper-timestamped)
- WhisperX limits in its own words: words with characters outside the alignment model's dictionary "cannot be aligned and therefore are not given a timing" (the examples are "2014." and "£13.60"); "Overlapping speech is not handled particularly well"; "Language specific wav2vec2 model is needed". **[E1]** — [WhisperX README](https://github.com/m-bain/whisperX)
- The current WhisperX code is more forgiving than its README: out-of-dictionary characters map to a wildcard so they still get times, and words left untimed are filled by "nearest" interpolation; a segment with no alignable characters keeps no word timings at all. **[E1]** — [WhisperX alignment.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/m-bain/whisperX/HEAD/whisperx/alignment.py)
- Forced alignment needs a normalised transcript (numbers and symbols as words) and handles fillers poorly. **[E4, a competing project's claim]** — [whisper-timestamped README](https://github.com/linto-ai/whisper-timestamped)

#### Languages other than English
- OpenAI's large-v3 chart (dated 6 November 2023) prints these word error rates: German 5.7% on Common Voice 15 and 4.9% on FLEURS; Romanian 10.8% and 8.2%; for comparison English 9.3% and 4.1%, French 10.8% and 5.3%, Spanish 4.7% and 2.8%, Italian 5.5% and 3.0%. I read these from the figure's embedded labels by rank order; the printed values track the large-v3 bars. **[E1, vendor's own evaluation; extraction is mine]** — [Whisper language-breakdown figure](https://github.com/openai/whisper/blob/main/language-breakdown.svg)
- An independent paper lists Whisper large-v3 at 5.46% on FLEURS and 6.25% on Common Voice for German. **[E3 with the above, snippet]** — [Voxtral paper](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2507.13264)
- A Romanian model card reports zero-shot Whisper large-v3 character error rates of 3.4% (FLEURS) and 3.18% (Common Voice 25). **[E4, snippet]** — [BuzzASR Romanian](https://huggingface.co/BuzzASR/romanian)
- OpenAI states the models "show strong ASR results in ~10 languages", that accuracy "is directly correlated with the amount of training data" in a language, and that only 17% of the 680,000 training hours are non-English audio with matching transcripts, spread over 98 languages. **[E1]** — [Whisper model card](https://github.com/openai/whisper/blob/main/model-card.md)
- WhisperX's default alignment models: German uses torchaudio's `VOXPOPULI_ASR_BASE_10K_DE` (one of five built-in languages); Romanian uses the community Hugging Face model `gigant/romanian-wav2vec2`. **[E1]** — [WhisperX alignment.py](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/m-bain/whisperX/HEAD/whisperx/alignment.py)
- CrisperWhisper's forced aligner supports English only and falls back to attention timings elsewhere; ghost-editor is tested only in English and Russian; HyperFrames' fallback model is English-only `small.en`. **[E1]** — [CrisperWhisper README](https://github.com/nyrahealth/CrisperWhisper), [ghost-editor README](https://github.com/kurbaitaev/ghost-editor), [HyperFrames audio reference](https://github.com/heygen-com/hyperframes/blob/main/skills/media-use/references/audio.md)
- Romanian fine-tunes of large-v3-turbo exist on Hugging Face. **[E4, snippet]** — [TransferRapid model](https://huggingface.co/TransferRapid/whisper-large-v3-turbo_ro)

### Inferences
- An error of 50–100 ms is 1.5 to 3 frames at 30 fps: tolerable for captions that highlight word by word, risky for cuts placed on a word boundary, which is why the careful pipelines pad cuts and snap to boundaries instead of trusting raw times.
- For Romanian, roughly one word in ten wrong on harder audio means a human proofreading pass on captions is not optional; German needs it less often. English brand and technical terms inside Romanian or German speech are a likely weak spot, though I found no measurement.
- Defaults in the tooling assume English (`medium.en`, `small.en`, `whisper-tiny`), so a Romanian or German pipeline must set a multilingual model and the language explicitly at every step.
- WhisperX is the best-documented route to tight word boundaries in German. For Romanian its community alignment model is of unknown quality, so compare it on real footage against cross-attention timings (faster-whisper, or stable-ts on top) before committing.
- Because hallucinations cluster in silence, running voice-activity detection first and removing dead air before transcription should reduce both invented words and stretched timestamps.

### Gaps
- No independent benchmark compares word-boundary error across openai-whisper, faster-whisper, whisper.cpp and mlx-whisper, and none measures word timing in Romanian or German; the planned search did not run.
- The only cross-system timing table comes from a vendor, on read English speech.
- Parakeet's language coverage (whether it handles Romanian and German) was not verified.
- arXiv and Hugging Face pages were seen as snippets only.

## 4. Getting reference videos to study: yt-dlp's current limits, what the platforms' terms say, legitimate alternatives, and which third-party tools find "outlier" videos

### Takeaway
yt-dlp still works but is fragile for YouTube in 2026, particularly from cloud machines: it needs an external JavaScript runtime, increasingly a "PO token", and its maintainers warn that using account cookies risks a ban, while practitioners report that bot checks follow data-centre IP addresses regardless of cookies. Legitimate routes give metadata, not files (TikTok Creative Center is free and public, the YouTube Data API allows about 100 searches a day by default, Meta Content Library is for vetted researchers only), and outlier finders cost about US$19–50 a month, with Sandcastles the one priced for Reels, TikTok and Shorts together.

### Cited Findings

#### yt-dlp in 2026
- The canonical report of "Sign in to confirm you're not a bot" was opened on 7 June 2024, is pinned and labelled as an external site issue, came from a user on proxies, and had duplicates folded into it through June 2026. **[E1]** — [yt-dlp issue #10128](https://github.com/yt-dlp/yt-dlp/issues/10128)

## 5. Which quality checks do professional editors run before delivery (loudness, safe zones, caption accuracy, flashes, frozen frames, sync)?

### Takeaway
Checklists converge on a loudness target of −14 LUFS integrated with a −1 dBTP true-peak ceiling (a house rule, not a published Meta requirement), keeping text out of platform interface zones whose pixel sizes differ by source, proofing captions against the transcript, and scanning for dead air, frozen or black frames and audio pops. FFmpeg ships detectors for all of the machine-checkable items, and open-source agent skills already run them in self-check loops; flash safety has a formal accessibility rule but I found no short-form-specific practice.

### Cited Findings

#### Loudness
- A video-ad QA checklist uses −14 LUFS and −1 dBTP and states this is its own house rule, since Meta's Reels specification lists the audio format but no loudness target. **[E4, snippet]** — [Turntake](https://turntake.app/learn/video-ad-qa-checklist)
- Other guides give −14 to −16 LUFS for Reels and TikTok, say louder mixes get turned down by the platform, or put the target near −16 LUFS; the platform figure is not settled. **[E3 on the range, conflicting on the number, snippet, best-match]** — [Mr. Vocal](https://mrvocal.com/posts/loudness-for-shorts), [Audio Gear Hub](https://audiogearhub.com/mix-audio-for-social-media/)
- video-use finishes with two-pass loudness normalisation to −14 LUFS and at most −1 dBTP and measures the result with `ebur128=peak=true`; it treats an end card 15 dB under the dialogue, or effects louder than speech, as bugs. **[E4, practitioner rule, opened]** — [video-use SKILL.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/browser-use/video-use/HEAD/SKILL.md)
- FFmpeg's `loudnorm` implements EBU R128 normalisation in one or two passes with targets for integrated loudness, loudness range and true peak (defaults −24 LUFS and −2 dBTP, so the targets must be set); `ebur128` measures momentary, short-term and integrated loudness. **[E1]** — [FFmpeg filters documentation](https://github.com/FFmpeg/FFmpeg/blob/master/doc/filters.texi)
- A QC tool from a daily production pipeline fails files quieter than −24 LUFS or louder than −10 LUFS, and notes that whole-file loudness missed a case where one voice sat 7–10 dB below the others. **[E4, practitioner tool, opened]** — [shorts-qc](https://github.com/DangTruong96/shorts-qc)

#### Safe zones and caption placement
- One guide puts TikTok's bottom dead zone at 320–350 px and Reels' at 310–450 px depending on whether audio attribution shows; another says no text in the bottom 210 px, top 150 px or right 120 px. Both say TikTok is tightest on the right edge and Reels at the bottom. **[E4 each, conflicting, snippet, best-match]** — [BlitzCut](https://blitzcutai.com/blog/best-caption-placement-short-form-video), [InfluenceFlow](https://influenceflow.io/resources/the-ultimate-social-media-video-specs-guide-2026-edition/)
- ghost-editor's automated self-check verifies caption size and that nothing covers the speaker's face; shorts-qc computes whether a caption line fits the frame width at the chosen font size after a real overflow that cut glyphs in half. **[E4, practitioner tools, opened]** — [ghost-editor README](https://github.com/kurbaitaev/ghost-editor), [shorts-qc](https://github.com/DangTruong96/shorts-qc)

#### Caption accuracy and sync
- One checklist suggests transcribing the finished mix and comparing its word timings with the captions to catch sync errors. **[E4, snippet, best-match]** — [Turntake](https://turntake.app/learn/video-ad-qa-checklist)
- video-use computes caption times on the output timeline (`output_time = word.start - segment_start + segment_offset`), allows only verbatim word-level transcripts, and burns captions last so overlays cannot hide them. **[E4, practitioner rule, opened]** — [video-use SKILL.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/browser-use/video-use/HEAD/SKILL.md)
- shorts-qc re-transcribes the output with faster-whisper to catch speech in the wrong language, and states that a pass means "nothing obvious," not proof. **[E4, opened]** — [shorts-qc](https://github.com/DangTruong96/shorts-qc)

#### Cuts, dead air, frozen and black frames
- FFmpeg defaults: `silencedetect` reports audio below −60 dB for 2 seconds; `freezedetect` reports no significant picture change for 2 seconds at a −60 dB noise floor; `blackdetect` reports 2 seconds of frames that are at least 98% black. All are adjustable. **[E1]** — [FFmpeg filters documentation](https://github.com/FFmpeg/FFmpeg/blob/master/doc/filters.texi)
- shorts-qc's production thresholds are tighter: fail on an internal silence gap over 2.5 seconds (measured at −40 dB), black runs over 1.5 seconds or frozen frames over 3.0 seconds, and an aspect ratio that does not match the expected one. **[E4, opened]** — [shorts-qc](https://github.com/DangTruong96/shorts-qc)
- video-use inspects a ±1.5-second window at every cut on the rendered file for a visual flash or jump, a waveform spike from an audio pop, hidden captions and misaligned overlays, compares the output duration with the edit list, samples the first and last 2 seconds, and stops after three passes. Because "the agent cannot listen", it must report meter readings instead of claiming the audio sounds right. **[E4, opened]** — [video-use SKILL.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/browser-use/video-use/HEAD/SKILL.md)
- MakeMyClip's `check` command flags cuts that may clip a word and describes itself as a flag, not a guarantee. **[E4, opened]** — [MakeMyClip editor README](https://github.com/MakeMyClip/editor)

#### Human approval
- ghost-editor shows a page with a frame at every cut for approval before finishing; cut-as-code requires approval in a browser timeline; a published creator workflow reviews stills and a cut list before the final render; video-use requires strategy confirmation before cutting and spawns a critic sub-agent for publishable work. **[E3 across independent projects, opened]** — [ghost-editor README](https://github.com/kurbaitaev/ghost-editor), [cut-as-code README](https://github.com/WhiteTowerAI/cut-as-code), [Bomx gist](https://gist.github.com/Bomx/c5a1a16904a90689bbe5d6ceae96e256), [video-use SKILL.md](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/browser-use/video-use/HEAD/SKILL.md)

#### Flashes
- WCAG 2.3.1 treats content as unsafe when it flashes more than three times in one second and the flashes are large and bright enough: a general flash is a luminance change of 20 cd/m² or more where the darker image is below 160 cd/m², and the size threshold is a 341 × 256 CSS-pixel area (about 10 degrees of view). Named test tools are the Trace Center's PEAT and the Harding FPA. **[E1]** — [W3C: Understanding Three Flashes or Below Threshold](https://github.com/w3c/wcag/blob/main/understanding/20/three-flashes-or-below-threshold.html)
- FFmpeg's `photosensitivity` filter reduces flashes ("to help users with epilepsy") over a 30-frame window; it is a mitigation, not a compliance test. **[E1]** — [FFmpeg filters documentation](https://github.com/FFmpeg/FFmpeg/blob/master/doc/filters.texi)

### Inferences
- A defensible pre-delivery gate for talking-head shorts: format and duration (1080×1920, matches the edit list); loudness measured on the final file against −14 LUFS and −1 dBTP; no internal silence, black or freeze beyond chosen thresholds; a frame check at every cut; captions inside the strictest safe zone across the three platforms; caption text proofread by a person against the transcript; a re-transcription of the final audio for sync; and one human watch-through with sound on a phone.
- Because platform safe-zone numbers conflict, design to the most conservative figures and confirm with a private test upload on each platform.
- White flash transitions are a stock element of this style (HyperFrames ships a `flash-through-white` block), so a simple rule of no more than three full-frame flashes in any second is a cheap safeguard even though no short-form source asks for it.
- Whole-file loudness hides imbalance between speech, music and effects, so per-section readings are worth the extra step when music is ducked under a voice.

### Gaps
- **Official safe-zone and loudness specifications** from Meta, TikTok and YouTube were not retrieved; the search did not run. From memory only, unverified: YouTube normalises playback toward about −14 LUFS.
- No checklist from a named professional short-form editor or agency was captured; the evidence is tool documentation, vendor checklists and open-source agent skills.
- No source addressed photosensitive-flash checks for social video, platform flash warnings, colour-range or codec delivery specifications, or audio/video sync tolerances in milliseconds.
