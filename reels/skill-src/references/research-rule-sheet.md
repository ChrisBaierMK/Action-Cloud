# Research rule sheet (web research of 24 November 2026)

This is the working table from the research report "Short-form video rules outrun their evidence". The full
report with every source link is in Chris's workspace at `reports/Short form video craft research.md`.

Read the table with its limits in mind. Five of the six research files behind it were compiled from web-search
summaries: the researchers could open almost no source page, so the web figures are "as relayed by search".
No large study of top videos and no platform pass mark exist in what was found. Where this table and
`style-sheet.md` disagree, the style sheet wins for Chris's videos: it rests on his own references.

## Evidence labels

Each figure carries one of these labels, strongest first. **Measured here** means the five Reels, a tool run in this workspace or a file in this repository. **Official** means a platform's or licensor's own documentation or research, and **official via press** means a platform statement reported by trade press. **Study** means a dataset with a stated sample; a vendor study is one whose publisher sells a related tool. **Practitioner** means advice repeated across guides with no published data, **single claim** means one vendor, tool or creator, and **calculated** means the researchers' arithmetic on the above. **Unsourced** numbers are named only so they can be dropped. "Page unconfirmed" marks a figure the search summary quoted without saying which result page carried it. Pixel positions refer to a 1080×1920 frame with y measured from the top. The table is the working rule sheet; the sections after it give the sources, conflicts and gaps behind each line.

## What the research could not answer

- Sound effects: types, frequency, any effect on retention.
- How long the speaker's face can stay off screen.
- Animation timings for graphics, and hook-title size and duration.
- Any organic safe zone published by a platform (only ad safe zones are official).
- Completion or retention by video length.
- Any comparison of formats (talking head, tutorial, story, faceless explainer).
- Which hooks or calls to action produce bookings or leads for a service or local business.
- What named top creators or editors actually use.
- Any tested workflow that serves long-form and short-form from one system.

## Findings worth remembering

- Instagram's head named watch time, likes and sends as the top three ranking signals (January 2025); sends matter slightly more for reaching non-followers. Judge a video against the account's own median, not against a universal number.
- Business Reels lose 60-65% of their views inside the first 3 seconds in one vendor dataset of about 140,000 Reels. The opening decides most of the outcome.
- Opening on the result or the product is the only hook pattern with large-sample support, and that support is one vendor's correlational data.
- Reels above 3 minutes lost 49.3% of reach in a dataset of 2.7 million Reels; below 90 seconds, length made little difference.
- No study isolates a lift from captions on Reels, TikTok or Shorts. "85% watch without sound" is a 2016 report about a few publishers on Facebook.
- Instagram said in June 2024 that content asking for "a specific word, number, or emoji" in comments may not be recommended, and also that using comments and messages for leads or sales is not engagement bait. A call to action should say what the viewer receives.
- Music: TikTok restricts businesses to its Commercial Music Library and Instagram withholds its licensed library from certain business accounts; each library is cleared for its own platform only.
- Only YouTube documents a loudness reference (-14 LUFS). Chris's five references measure -14.2 to -14.8 LUFS.
